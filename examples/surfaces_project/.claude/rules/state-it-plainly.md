# State It Plainly

Portable rule for all writing in this workflow. It governs body prose. Its
companion, `no-implied-predicate-titles.md`, governs headings, captions, and
other display lines. A caption's opening naming phrase belongs to the companion.
The full sentences inside a caption are governed by this rule. Copy both into a
new project's `.claude/rules/` and reference them from that project's
`CLAUDE.md`.

Added 18 September 2026 at Noel's direction.

---

## The device

The paper narrates itself. Instead of reporting what was done and what was
found, a sentence announces what is coming, tells the reader how much to value
it, or withholds a result for a beat so that its arrival lands harder.

The giveaway is that the sentence is about the work rather than about the
finding. It could have been written by someone who had not done the research.

## Two tests

Clear the sentence against "What this does not touch" at the end of this file
first. Then apply both tests.

1. **The delete test.** Cut the sentence. If no fact is lost, it was staging.
2. **The outsider test.** Could a person who had not done the work write this
   sentence? A sentence about how careful, direct, important, or falsifiable the
   research is fails, because its content is a posture rather than a result.

## Barred outright

Five species. The fifth was added after the other four, when it turned up in
the paper at paragraph length despite the rule.

### 1. Announcing the speech act instead of performing it

"One further loosening is worth stating." "Two objections deserve a direct
answer." "What a coercive title looks like is worth showing." "One feature of
the series deserves a plain statement."

If it were not worth stating, it would not be in the paper. Announcing
directness is the opposite of being direct. Delete the announcement and begin
with the thing itself.

### 2. Claiming the virtue rather than letting it show

"Each is a statement about the print record that can be true or false." "…so
that the results carry the discipline of pre-commitment."

A prediction that can be checked demonstrates as much by being checkable. A
pre-registered design is evidenced by the dated document, not by a clause
admiring it. State the procedure; let the reader draw the conclusion.

### 3. Stage directions and significance markers

"Panel B is the exhibit we most want a reader of exposure regressions to see."
"The exhibit is the paper's thesis in miniature." "The results arrive in the
order the framework predicts them." "This figure is the paper's opening fact."

These tell the reader what to feel about the content in place of content. The
exhibit either earns the reader's attention or it does not, and a sentence
asserting that it does adds nothing either way.

### 4. The trailing flourish

An interpretive claim delivered as an appositive or absolute phrase bolted to
the end of a sentence, rather than as a sentence of its own.

"…ten of them in Frankfurt and three in Strasbourg, a steady seller between
outbreaks." "…the accusation against the Jews of Sternberg, anti-Jewish violence
as breaking news before 1500." "…the justice done upon five hundred witches, the
story crossing into another language market."

This is the reveal in miniature, and it is the prose cousin of the device
`no-implied-predicate-titles.md` bars in headings. The claim is usually a real
one. Make it in a sentence, where it can be qualified and checked.

The withheld beat below is also a reveal, and it is rationed rather than barred
because its payoff is a sentence. A flourish has no verb, so there is nothing in
it to qualify or check.

### 5. Narrating the research process

"The hypothesis of 29 July predicted that plague would move print toward
practical content, the plan of 30 July specified flat leads, and the estimate
was committed ten minutes after that plan was approved." "The four predictions
were recorded as gates before the estimates that test them." "This is the
heterogeneity our own framework predicted before we estimated it." "The rule,
fixed before any table was made, is that the text quotes the wider standard
error."

Noel's ruling of 18 September 2026, given in the strongest terms: the paper
does not refer to what the authors predicted, when they predicted it, or how
the outcome compared with what they expected. That includes pre-registration
claims, dated planning documents, stop rules, gates, commit histories, and
any clause of the form "fixed in advance" or "before we estimated." Such
sentences are about the project's notebook rather than its evidence, they read
as defensive, and they invite a referee to audit the process instead of the
result. The model's predictions are a different thing entirely: "Proposition 3
says local production should stay flat, and it does" states what a theory
implies and what the data show, and is permitted. The line is whether the
subject of the sentence is the model or the authors.

The rule reaches everything (Noel, 18 September 2026, on being asked whether
it stopped short of the self-implication framing). "This project began as
exactly the design it corrects," "the founding hypothesis of this project was
that chain," "we speak from inside the practice," and "the question this
project began with" are all barred. The paper's origin, the authors' earlier
designs, and the failure that motivated the framework are the notebook too.
Write the design and what it produces: "A design that reads persecutory print
by city and year as local persecutory culture produces the aggregate
co-movement and fails every local test." 

## Permitted in moderation

Noel's ruling of 18 September 2026: these two are acceptable used sparingly and
tiresome used often. The budgets make "sparingly" checkable.

### The withheld beat

Posing a prediction, then confirming it in a short sentence. "Proposition 3 says
its local production should stay flat. It does."

- **Budget: at most one per section.**
- Earned only where the setup is a claim the reader has been tracking and the
  payoff is a genuine result. Never as decoration on a routine number, and never
  as a teaser that withholds a result the sentence could simply have given.

### Signposting

Telling the reader where something lives or what a later section will do.
"Section 6 estimates the supply gradient."

- **Budget: cross-section references are free. A sentence announcing what the
  current paragraph or subsection is about to do is not**, because the heading
  has already done that work.
- The current draft runs four to seventeen cross-section references per section,
  which is the intended level for a nine-section paper. Pointing at another
  section is navigation, and a long argument needs a lot of it.

## Wrong → right (from real edits in this repo)

- *wrong:* "Each is a statement about the print record that can be true or
  false, and we fixed all four before estimating any of them."
  *right:* cut it. The opening of Section 3 already states the pre-commitment and
  a footnote documents it, so the sentence repeated a fact and admired it.
- *wrong:* "What a coercive title looks like is worth showing."
  *right:* "Six titles show what the coercive tier holds."
- *wrong:* "One feature of the plague-specific series deserves a plain
  statement."
  *right:* "The plague-specific series has one weakness."
- *wrong:* "Two objections deserve a direct answer. The first is that registry
  locations are measured with error."
  *right:* "The first of two objections is that registry locations are measured
  with error."
- *wrong:* "Panel B is the exhibit we most want a reader of exposure regressions
  to see. It replaces the count…"
  *right:* "Panel B replaces the count…"
- *wrong:* "…the accusation against the Jews of Sternberg, anti-Jewish violence
  as breaking news before 1500."
  *right:* "…reports the host-desecration accusation against the Jews of
  Sternberg. Anti-Jewish violence was breaking news before 1500."

## What this does not touch

1. **Substantive reasons.** "A miss correlated with the treatment would bias the
   estimate, and a miss uncorrelated with it would attenuate." That is an
   argument about why a test was built, not a claim about the author's care.
2. **Honest reports of surprise or failure.** "The estimate falls to 0.009, and
   the earlier version was an artifact." Reporting that something turned out
   otherwise is a finding.
3. **Enumeration.** "The first prediction concerns a local shock to demand."
   Numbering is navigation, provided the content follows immediately.
4. **Stating a limitation.** "The design cannot rule out a moderate effect."
   Naming what the evidence will not carry is content.
